## Writing code fast no longer matters as much

Lately, building tools with LLM APIs at work, I've noticed something. There
was a time when "how fast can you ship this feature" was one of the
measures of a developer's skill. That measure itself is shaking now. Typing
speed, memorized syntax, how well you know an API's docs — none of these
set you apart from other developers anymore. AI has closed most of that
gap.

That doesn't mean the job of "developer" has become unnecessary. If
anything, the opposite: the role of deciding *what* to build and *whether*
it's the right direction is becoming more visible than ever.

## What's left: the ability to define the problem

Once you start handing work to AI, you learn quickly. AI is remarkably fast
at solving a given problem, but defining what the real problem actually is
still falls to a human. When requirements are vague, when stakeholders want
different things, when you have to separate what needs solving now from
what can wait — these judgment calls can't be replaced by a few lines of a
prompt.

That's part of why I've made a habit of defining failure conditions before
starting anything. Defining "what counts as failure" first makes clear both
the scope of what AI should handle and the points a human needs to review.
If the problem definition is fuzzy, AI's output can look plausible while
still pointing in the wrong direction entirely.

## What AI can't take over: judgment and accountability

No one ships AI-written code straight to production (and no one should).
Someone still has to judge — and be accountable for — whether that code is
correct, whether it's safe in this service's context, how it behaves in
edge cases. That accountability is not something you can delegate to AI.

And, ironically, making that judgment well requires even stronger
fundamentals. If you don't understand how the browser renders, what happens
when a network request fails, or where things go wrong when state
management breaks down, you can't properly verify what AI hands you. That's
exactly why the people who use AI well are the ones who can't afford to
skip the fundamentals.

## What it comes down to: the habit of verifying

These days, the skill I care about most isn't speed — it's the habit of
verification.

- Not taking AI's suggested code at face value, and asking why it was
  written that way
- Checking whether the result actually passes the failure conditions I
  defined
- Telling the difference between something built fast and something built
  right

No tool, however good it gets, does this for you. If anything, as AI makes
output faster, the gap in how well people verify that output will show up
even more clearly in the quality of what ships.

## Takeaway

If I had to name one skill developers need in the AI era, it would be
judgment: deciding what counts as the real problem, how much to trust what
AI produces, and whether you can stand behind that decision. I think this
is a moment to keep training the mind that makes these calls, not just the
hands that write the code.
