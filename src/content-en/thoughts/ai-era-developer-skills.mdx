![My desk these days: AI handles the coding while I ponder metrics and users](/images/ai-era-hero.webp)

At my previous company, I spent a lot of time designing, writing code, performing QA, and analyzing data. Since it was a collaboration tool, the domain was more complex than my current company's. Even after joining my current company, up until October of last year, AI handled many simple tasks, but software design was still an area that required a lot of my own deep thinking.

Now, however, things are different. If I paste a well-written spec document onto a solid design system and clean existing codebase, AI automatically handles the implementation based on the existing code. There's no need to manually adjust much, nor do I have to write E2E test code myself. Software development has almost become boring.

Perhaps that's why, whereas my pre-AI development flow followed a fixed sequence of "Planning → Design → Development → QA", my post-AI flow is shifting toward "Development → Requesting Planning or Design". As development becomes easier, developers naturally shift their attention elsewhere. In the past, I worked overtime writing code to meet deadlines; now, since AI handles "coding and testing", I find myself more interested in product and data-driven questions like "What is the goal of this feature?" or "Why aren't users using this feature much?"

![Comparing development flows before and after AI](/images/dev-flow-before-after.svg)

In fact, the widget feature I deployed recently isn't being used by users as much as expected, prompting me to reflect on why. The Cafe24 app I launched with colleagues from my previous company met a similar fate. We released it expecting it to succeed, but the response was underwhelming. That's why I've been reading *Lean Analytics* lately.

A central concept emphasized throughout the book is **OMTM** (One Metric That Matters). The authors state that data is meaningful only when it helps determine the next action. Although I had looked at many impressive-sounding numbers in the past (vanity metrics like total visitors or page views), I had never deeply considered what stage my service was currently in or what single metric I needed to focus on at that stage. The book explains that a business progresses through stages: Empathy → Stickiness → Virality → Revenue → Scale, and the key metrics change with each stage. For both the widget and the Cafe24 app, I had finished "building and shipping," but I hadn't figured out where they currently stood or what metrics I should actually track.

However, talking to other developers recently, it seems many have similarly broadened their focus to wider domains.

Therefore, I don't believe developers will disappear in the AI era. Instead, I think we will become much more generalist—not just people who write code, but individuals who seamlessly move between planning, analyzing data, and translating those insights back into code. In my next post, I plan to share how I actually applied the metrics I learned from *Lean Analytics* to the widget and the Cafe24 app.
